# Forensic Analysis and Detection of AI-Generated Content

## 1. Introduction

The rapid development of generative artificial intelligence has made it increasingly easy to create synthetic text, images, audio, and video. These technologies have legitimate applications, but they can also be misused to produce misleading content, impersonate individuals, and manipulate digital evidence.

Digital media forensics uses computational methods to examine content, identify possible manipulation, and assess its origin. However, no single detection technique can reliably identify every form of AI-generated content. Detection systems should therefore combine multiple signals and communicate uncertainty clearly.

This report reviews common approaches to detecting AI-generated and manipulated content across different media types.

## 2. Detection of AI-Generated Text

Large language models generate text by predicting tokens based on patterns learned during training. Some statistical and linguistic properties of generated text can be useful for detection.

### Common analysis methods

**Perplexity and burstiness**

Perplexity measures how predictable a sequence of text is under a language model. Burstiness describes variation in sentence structure and length. These measurements can provide useful statistical features, but human and AI-generated writing can overlap considerably.

**Linguistic pattern analysis**

Repeated phrases, unusually consistent sentence structures, and certain stylistic patterns may be examined as part of a broader classification system. These characteristics are not unique to AI-generated writing.

**Machine learning classification**

Supervised classifiers can be trained on human-written and AI-generated examples. Their performance depends on the training data, writing domain, language, and generator models represented in the dataset.

### Limitations

Text detection is particularly difficult when content is short, edited, translated, or generated by models that were not represented in the training data. A detector's output should be treated as an estimate rather than proof of authorship.

## 3. Detection of AI-Generated Audio

Synthetic audio can be produced through text-to-speech systems, voice conversion, and voice cloning. Audio forensics examines acoustic characteristics and possible synthesis artifacts.

### Common analysis methods

**Spectral analysis**

Spectrograms and frequency-domain features can reveal unusual patterns, artifacts, or inconsistencies in a recording. These signals may also arise from compression, recording equipment, or post-processing.

**Prosody and speech characteristics**

Pitch variation, rhythm, pauses, pronunciation, and speaking rate can be examined. Unnatural transitions or inconsistent speech characteristics may provide useful clues, but modern speech synthesis can reproduce many natural variations.

**Acoustic and neural classification**

Machine learning models can learn patterns that distinguish examples of synthetic and genuine recordings. Models must be evaluated against different speakers, languages, recording conditions, and synthesis systems.

**Speaker comparison**

When an authorized reference recording is available, speaker characteristics can be compared. This can help investigate impersonation, but speaker similarity alone does not establish whether a recording is genuine.

### Limitations

Background noise, telephone compression, low-quality recordings, and changes in speaking style can affect detection. Results should not be treated as definitive evidence of identity or authenticity.

## 4. Detection of AI-Generated Images

Image forensics examines visual and statistical characteristics that may indicate synthetic generation or manipulation.

### Common analysis methods

**Spatial artifact analysis**

Convolutional neural networks and other image classifiers can learn patterns associated with particular synthetic image datasets or generation methods.

**Frequency-domain analysis**

Transforming an image into the frequency domain can reveal statistical patterns that are less obvious in the spatial representation. These patterns may help distinguish certain generated images from natural photographs.

**Visual consistency analysis**

Investigators may examine lighting, shadows, reflections, textures, edges, and object geometry for inconsistencies. These observations are useful clues but can also occur in ordinary photographs.

**Metadata inspection**

Image metadata may contain information about capture devices, editing software, timestamps, and processing history. Metadata can be missing, modified, or deliberately removed, so it should not be considered conclusive evidence.

### Limitations

Image generation systems continue to improve, and image editing or recompression can weaken detector performance. Models trained on older generators may not generalize to newer systems.

## 5. Detection of AI-Generated and Manipulated Video

Video analysis is more complex than still-image analysis because it involves both individual frames and changes over time.

### Common analysis methods

**Frame-level analysis**

Individual frames can be examined using image-forensic techniques to identify possible synthetic content or facial manipulation.

**Temporal consistency**

Investigators can examine changes in facial features, object boundaries, lighting, and motion across consecutive frames. Inconsistencies may indicate manipulation, although ordinary video compression and motion blur can produce similar artifacts.

**Biological signal analysis**

Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) estimates pulse-related changes in skin color from video. Research has explored using these signals to identify certain manipulated videos. Its reliability depends on video quality, lighting, movement, and the manipulation method.

**Deep learning detection**

CNN-based and video-transformer-based models can learn spatial and temporal patterns from datasets containing genuine and manipulated videos.

### Limitations

Video detectors may struggle with low resolution, heavy compression, unfamiliar manipulation techniques, and changes in recording conditions. Biological signals are not universally reliable and should be treated as one possible source of evidence.

## 6. Code and AI-Generated Code Analysis

AI-assisted programming tools can generate source code, complete functions, and modify existing software. Distinguishing AI-generated code from human-written code is difficult because both can follow the same programming conventions.

### Possible analysis methods

**Code structure analysis**

Abstract syntax trees (ASTs) can represent the structural organization of source code. Structural features, naming patterns, and repeated code constructs can be studied by classifiers.

**Style and complexity analysis**

Formatting, identifier naming, function length, code complexity, and repetition can be examined as statistical features. These characteristics are not exclusive to AI-generated code.

**Similarity and provenance analysis**

Code similarity analysis can identify overlap with known codebases or supplied reference material. It is useful for investigating duplication, but similarity does not independently establish authorship.

### Limitations

Programming languages, frameworks, formatting tools, and developer conventions strongly influence code structure. A code detector should not claim that code was generated by AI solely because it follows common programming patterns.

## 7. Embeddings and Provenance-Related Signals

Embeddings are numerical representations of text, images, audio, or other data. They can be used to compare content and identify similarities.

### Possible analysis methods

**Embedding similarity**

A model can convert content into vectors and calculate similarity between representations. This can support duplicate detection, clustering, and comparison against known reference samples.

**Reference-based comparison**

Embeddings may be compared with a labelled reference dataset to investigate whether content resembles known examples. The result depends on the quality and coverage of that dataset.

**Provenance information**

Provenance refers to information about where content originated and how it has changed. It may include capture details, editing history, creator information, or cryptographically signed assertions.

### Limitations

An embedding is not an authorship certificate. Similarity to a reference dataset does not prove that a specific model generated the content. Provenance information can also be incomplete or unavailable.

## 8. Watermark and Content Credential Analysis

Watermark analysis investigates whether content contains signals associated with a particular marking or provenance system.

### Common approaches

**Visible and invisible watermarks**

Some systems add visible marks, while others embed signals that are intended to be detected computationally. Invisible watermarks may be weakened by cropping, compression, resizing, or other transformations.

**Metadata and embedded credentials**

Content may include metadata or credentials describing its creation and editing history. Their presence and validity should be examined separately.

**C2PA and Content Credentials**

The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) defines a technical standard for attaching verifiable provenance information to digital content. A valid credential can support verification of signed claims and recorded actions. It does not automatically prove that the content is truthful, human-created, or unmanipulated.

### Limitations

Not all content carries watermarks or credentials. Their absence does not prove that content is AI-generated, and their presence does not independently guarantee authenticity.

## 9. Evaluation and Reliability

Detection performance should be evaluated using representative datasets and clearly defined metrics.

| Metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Proportion of all predictions that are correct |
| Precision | Proportion of positive predictions that are correct |
| Recall | Proportion of actual positive examples detected |
| F1-score | Balance between precision and recall |
| False positive rate | Frequency of genuine content incorrectly flagged |
| False negative rate | Frequency of generated or manipulated content missed |

Testing should include data from multiple sources, generators, recording environments, languages, and compression conditions. Evaluation should also consider whether the model performs well on data that was not used during training.

## 10. Conclusion

AI-generated content detection is an evolving area of digital forensics. Text, image, audio, video, code, embedding, and watermark analysis each provide different forms of evidence, but every method has limitations.

A reliable forensic platform should combine independent analysis techniques where appropriate, validate its models against relevant datasets, and distinguish between detection results and provenance evidence.

Most importantly, detection scores should be presented as estimates with clear explanations and limitations. No automated detector should claim certainty about the origin of digital content based on a single signal.
